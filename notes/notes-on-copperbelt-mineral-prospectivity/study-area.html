The Study Area Is a Geological Argument

Where the Copperbelt is, where the data are, and why the two polygons are not the same.

The standard map of the Copperbelt has a line down the middle of it. The line is the international border. It has nothing to do with the geology and everything to do with which files you are allowed to open. I have been suspicious of study-area polygons ever since.

The belt is a geological object. It is the Katangan Basin and its Lufilian deformation: the Roan Group, the Mines Series, the Ore Shale, the evaporite-related breccias, and the thrusts and folds that repeat the sequence. The copper and cobalt are stratabound in the lower Roan. The Congolese and Zambian Copperbelts are not two belts. They are one belt with a border through it. A study area that stops at the border is a study of two archives, not of one mineral system.

The data stop at the border too, and for reasons that have nothing to do with geology. Tenure, reporting rules, the language of the assessment reports, the density of drilling, the age of the geological maps, and the availability of airborne surveys all change at the line. If the border is the edge of the study area, those differences are built into the model. The model will learn that one side of the line is more prospective. It will be right about the archive and wrong about the belt.

The data footprint

The second polygon is the footprint of the predictors: where the aeromagnetic survey flew, where the gravity data exist, where the geological map is at 1:50,000 rather than 1:2,000,000, where the regolith is thin enough for the geochemistry to mean something. That polygon is smaller than the belt, and it is not a random sample of it. In this project the aeromagnetic coverage has large gaps, and some of those gaps sit over known deposits. A model trained on that grid can separate cells by survey coverage as easily as by geology. The magnetic coefficient then has no geological meaning, because the predictor is partly a map of where the aircraft flew.

The third polygon is the one the model is allowed to speak about. It is the grid: 1,899 cells, 139 known deposits. The cell size is a statement about the scale at which you are willing to make a prediction. If the cell is 10 km across and the deposits are 2 km apart, you have already decided that two deposits and a barren patch can be the same observation. That may be acceptable for regional screening. It is not acceptable for a mine-scale target. The grid is not a neutral container. It is the resolution of the claim.

What is a negative?

Then there is the question that decides whether any of this works. In a belt with 139 known deposits, the other 1,760 cells are not barren. They are cells with no known deposit. Some are under Kalahari cover. Some are in the footwall or the hanging wall of the Roan. Some are known prospects that have never been drilled. Some are simply where nobody has looked. The model does not know the difference, and if you give it a predictor that correlates with exploration effort, it will learn the effort and call it geology.

This is why I am suspicious of a study area defined by a buffer around known deposits, by a tenure block, or by a country. The geometry of the known deposits is the geometry of the exploration history. If the study area is drawn to include the known camps and exclude the gaps, the model is being asked to rediscover the camps. It will do that. The AUC will be respectable. The map will be useless.

Three polygons, one map

So what is the right framework? I would use three polygons, and I would show all three on the same map.

The first is the geological belt: the Katangan Basin and the Lufilian Arc, with the Roan sub-basins and the major thrust systems. That is the frame in which the mineral system makes sense, and it is the frame in which the tectonic domains of Daly et al. are a useful stratification, provided the domain boundaries are treated as interpretations and not as facts.

The second is the data footprint, with the gaps shown explicitly. Not a mask that silently removes the gaps, but a map that says where the predictors exist and where they do not. The gaps are not missing values to be filled. They are places where the model has nothing to say.

The third is the grid and the spatial blocks, with the cell size and the block size justified against the deposit spacing and the range of the spatial dependence. If the blocks are smaller than the spacing between camps, the cross-validation is interpolation. If they are larger than the belt itself, it is not a spatial test at all.

If those three polygons coincide, the problem is clean. In the Copperbelt they do not. The belt continues under cover along strike. The data do not. The known deposits cluster where the Roan is exposed and where the explorers went. The model sees the clusters. The border cuts the archive in half. The geology does not care. The honest thing is to say where the polygons differ and what that does to the conclusion.

What I would put in the paper

A study area is not a preliminary. It is a statement about what you think the belt is, where you have information, and where you are entitled to make a claim. If you do not write that statement down, the model will make it for you, and it will make it badly.

The map should show the known, the unknown, and the untested. The last category is the one that matters, and it is the one that most prospectivity maps leave out. A cell with no known deposit is not a negative until you can say why. If the reason is that nobody looked, the cell belongs in the same category as the aeromagnetic gap: a place where the model has no evidence, not a place where it has evidence of absence.

That is the argument I would make in the text. The belt is the geological frame. The data footprint is what the evidence covers. The grid is the resolution of the claim. They are three different things, and the study area is the intersection of them, stated explicitly. Anything else is a polygon that flatters the model.
