A 0.7 AUC Is Not a Prospectivity Map

What a respectable ROC score means when the positive class is 139 cells and the negatives are not barren rock.

An AUC of 0.7 sounds like a pass mark. In a belt with 139 known deposits in 1,899 cells, it is a much weaker statement than it looks. It says that a randomly chosen known deposit tends to score above a randomly chosen cell. Most cells are not deposits. Most of them are not even prospective. Clearing that bar is not the same as finding ore.

What the negatives are

The first thing to get straight is what the negative class is. The 1,760 cells without a known deposit are not 1,760 barren cells. They are cells where no deposit is recorded. Some are under cover. Some are in the wrong stratigraphy, which is a real negative. Some are known prospects, anomalies, or occurrences that have never been drilled. Some are in areas nobody has mapped at a useful scale. The model is not separating mineralized rock from barren rock. It is separating known deposits from everything else in the archive. That is an easier problem, and it is contaminated by the exploration history.

Two curves, two questions

ROC-AUC is the probability that a deposit outranks a non-deposit:

\[ \mathrm{AUC} = P\!\left(\hat{p}^{+} > \hat{p}^{-}\right). \]

It gives every positive–negative pair the same weight. With 139 positives and 1,760 negatives there are 244,640 pairs, and the vast majority involve negatives that are easy to separate: granite, cover, post-orogenic sediments. A model can get a respectable AUC by learning the first-order geology and never learn where the ore is within the Katangan.

Average precision, the area under the precision–recall curve and the number usually reported as PR-AUC, is different:

\[ \mathrm{AP} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}} \mathrm{Precision}(i). \]

It concentrates on the top of the ranking, which is the part a targeting exercise uses. Its no-skill value is the prevalence, not 0.5:

\[ \mathrm{AP}_{\text{no skill}} \approx \pi = \frac{139}{1899} \approx 0.073. \]

An average precision of 0.15 would be about twice random. That is not a good absolute score, but it is not zero, and the raw number cannot be read without the baseline. A reviewer who knows the belt will ask for the baseline before they ask for the score.

Why the AUC is high

The AUC is high for a geological reason. The belt has a strong first-order contrast: the Roan host rocks against the basement, the cover, and the post-Katangan intrusions. A model with distance to lithological contacts, distance to faults, and a gravity or magnetic response will pick up that contrast. It will rank the Katangan above the granite. That is real geology, and it is worth knowing. But it is a district-scale statement. It does not tell you which part of the Roan is mineralized. The PR curve is where that distinction shows up.

Then there is the spatial version of the same problem. If you pool the predictions from all the blocks and compute one AUC, you are comparing positives and negatives across blocks as well as within them. A model that gives a higher score to a block with more deposits gains AUC from the between-block comparison. That is not within-block discrimination. It is the model learning the regional base rate. The pooled AUC can look better than any of the per-block AUCs. Report both, and say which is which. The mean of the per-block AUCs is the more honest number for transfer.

Block size matters for the same reason. If the blocks are 50 km across and the deposits are 5 km apart, the training and test sets may share the same camp. The test is then interpolation, not transfer. The block size should be chosen against the range of the spatial dependence and the spacing of the deposits, not against the bounding box.

What I want to see

What I want to see is not a single number. I want the map, the capture curve, and the names. If the top 50 cells contain 15 of the 139 deposits, that is a fourfold lift over the 3.7 you would get by picking 50 cells at random. That is a result worth discussing. If the top 50 contain five, the model is not a targeting tool, whatever the AUC says. I want to know which deposits were missed. If Tenke-Fungurume or Konkola or Nchanga is in the bottom half of the ranking, the model has a problem that no amount of AUC will fix. I want to know what the false positives are. If they are in the same stratigraphic unit as the true positives, the model may be onto something. If they are in the granite, it is not.

I also want the per-block results, not the pooled one. The pooled number hides the folds where the model failed. A model that gets 0.75 in four blocks and 0.55 in one is a different object from a model that gets 0.69 everywhere. The first has a transfer problem; the second has a ceiling problem. Both matter, but they call for different work.

The number is not the result

The 0.7 AUC is not a lie. It is a statement that the model has learned something about the belt. The low average precision is a statement that what it learned is not concentrated enough to be a deposit-scale targeting tool. Both can be true. The mistake is to report the first without the second, or to report the second without the baseline.

A 0.7 AUC is a reasonable first pass at a district-scale screening problem. It is not a prospectivity map. The map is the map: the high scores in the right stratigraphy, the right structure, and the right part of the belt; the gaps visible; the false positives explained or admitted; the missed deposits named. If the map cannot be defended in front of someone who has drilled the belt, the AUC cannot save it.

I have not yet separated the within-block signal from the between-block base rate. That is the next test. Until it is done, I treat the 0.7 as a reason to keep looking, not as a result.
